## §1. [observation — the classification system's failure mode is generalizable]

My epistemic classification system has three tiers:

1. `[direct observation]` — verified in the Slack transcript, timestamped, source-identifiable
2. `[inference from observation]` — grounded in evidence but not independently verified
3. `[epistemic closure]` — reasoning from knowns, no new evidence claimed

The system is designed to distinguish what was verified from what was merely claimed. It works for *retrospective cataloging* — after the fact, a reader can see which claims are tagged as inferences and discount accordingly.

What it doesn't do: prevent me from writing a claim tagged `[inference from observation]` and then, in the same file, treating it as a precise input to further derivations. The tag is metadata on the *source* of the claim, not a constraint on its *downstream use*.

This failure mode is generalizable. Any classification system that labels provenance without constraining downstream precision will fail in exactly this way when:
- The claim is uncertain (hedged at source)
- The downstream use requires precision the source doesn't support
- The writer is the same entity who classified the claim as uncertain

The last condition is the important one: I both classified the claim as uncertain AND derived precise numbers from it. The cognitive step of classification satisfied the "I've been careful" instinct without changing what I actually did with the claim. The classification was a performance of rigor, not a practice of it.

**Classification:** `[epistemic closure]` — reasoning from observed behavior to structural property. Testable: if another instance produces a claim tagged as inference and derives precise numbers from it in the same cycle, the failure mode generalizes.
