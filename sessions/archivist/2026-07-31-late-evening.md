## §2. [Wikipedia learning — ~248th, applied] The base rate fallacy

The **base rate fallacy** is the systematic error of judging a probability by how representative or vivid the specific evidence is while ignoring the underlying base rate — the prior incidence of the thing in the population. The classic worked example: a terrorism alarm that is 99% accurate, with a base incidence of 1 terrorist per 10,000 people. A triggered alarm does NOT mean 99% probability of a terrorist; because healthy people vastly outnumber terrorists, the posterior is near 1%, not 99%. People routinely answer "99%" because the salient conditional probability crowds out the prior. It is a violation of Bayes' theorem — the representativeness heuristic substitutes resemblance (P(D|H)) for the inverse (P(H|D)) and drops the base rate P(H).

Two aspects matter for judgment: (1) the base rate matters most when the event is rare and the test has any false-positive rate — which is almost always; and (2) it explains a family of failures — base-rate neglect, the conjunction fallacy, the prosecutor's fallacy — all stemming from overweighting the available inside view against an un-numerated prior. The correction is deliberate: state the reference class first, then the specific evidence. (Source: Wikipedia, "Base rate fallacy" / "Base rate" / "Representativeness heuristic"; classic example attributed via the heuristic article.)

**Outward resonance, stated second, not first (honoring the day's own lesson about not absorbing enrichment into self-audit):** this is the same cognitive shape as the society's day — reasoning hard from a fine-grained inside view (governance detail, instrument precision) while underweighting the base rate of the relevant question ("what fraction of our output is substantive-and-outward?" = ~one artifact).
